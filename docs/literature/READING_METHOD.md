# How to read and classify the papers: Dr. Hammam's method

Written 11 November 2026 from Dr. Hammam Al-Ghamdi's lecture on the literature review
(research methods, chapter 3), recorded in two parts: part 1 on 23 February 2026
(25 min 25 s), part 2 on 24 February 2026 (16 min 7 s). The slides are in English
and the talk is in Arabic. This file is the team's working summary of the method,
for reading the 40 sources of `TOP40.md`. It is not a copy of the lecture.

**How it was made.** Both videos were transcribed on Jad's PC with faster-whisper
large-v3 (language Arabic, run locally), and the 24 slides were read from frames of
the video. Times are `mm:ss` from the start of each part's video, so they point
into the recording. The transcript is a machine transcript: a few words are
misheard, and where a word below rests on a guess the guess is said. The
transcripts are not in the repository, since they hold the voices of students
who are not on this team.

## The idea in one paragraph

A literature review is **not a summary** (part 1, 02:25-02:55). Beginners think it
is: "researcher one said this, researcher two did that." Dr. Hammam's verbs are
describe, summarize, **evaluate** (say whether a study is good, and why),
clarify and **integrate** (link one idea to another) (01:02-01:54). Two people
writing about the same papers write different reviews, because the evaluation and
the links are theirs (01:57-02:20). The review ends in the **gap**, what is known
and what is not (03:59-04:30), and the gap is what the methodology then fills. A
gap the methodology does not address is the wrong gap to write down
(07:57-08:50).

His picture of it (04:55-06:04): you are flown over a city you have never seen,
in a helicopter. You see which districts are crowded and which are empty, and
you decide where you should live. The city is the published work; where you
live is the placement of your own work.

## The five stages (part 1, slide 8, 11:46)

The stages loop. Stage III sends you back to I and II until the picture is
complete (20:21-20:29).

### Stage I: annotated bibliography (slide 9, from 12:13)

- Read each source and write **"a brief critical synopsis"** of it (slide 9).
- While reading, mark each paper related or not related, and save every related
  one. Record for each: the authors, the year, the method it used, and a summary
  (12:27-12:49).
- **How to read one paper** (13:25-13:45): first read the **abstract and the
  conclusion**, and decide from them whether it is related. If it is, go
  straight to the **methodology** (or the specific part you need), to understand
  it and summarise it.
- Keep the summaries in one place you return to and add to (12:57-13:23). He
  says to keep a sheet that summarises the papers (13:42-13:45; the transcript
  reads «يكسل شيء», most likely "an Excel sheet"). The tracker is that sheet.

### Stage II: thematic organization (slide 10, from 13:47)

- With enough reading you start to notice "I read something like this four or
  five papers ago", and you begin to group similar papers under umbrellas
  (13:46-14:14).
- What makes a group (14:14-14:42): the papers **share their data**, or **use the
  same method**, or **discuss the same problem** (or the same idea for a different
  problem or country). Themes come out of the similarities and the differences
  (14:42-14:54). On the slide: themes, subthemes or categories, arranged
  chronologically within a theme, with coherence between them.
- **If you cannot see the themes: the elevator pitch** (14:55-16:43). Imagine
  meeting your supervisor in a lift: "You read fifty papers? In ten seconds,
  what did you find?" Under that pressure you sort the fifty into groups, in
  your own view. That sorting is the theme list.

### Stage III: more reading (slides 11-12, from 16:50)

- Reading is the main work, and it is read with an analytical and critical eye
  (16:57-17:11).
- His picture (17:17-18:29): a jigsaw. Each piece is a paper. Put the pieces
  where they go, and the missing pieces show. A missing piece is a possible gap,
  and you check it by reading more **in that specific area**.
- The aim is to separate what is discovered from what is **not discovered yet**.
  If you confirm that nobody has done it, it is a gap (19:12-19:30).
- On the slide: look for more work by the same authors and on the same methods,
  set aside areas that turned out less relevant, and integrate the new reading
  into the draft (19:49-20:12).

### Stage IV: write individual sections (slides 13-14, from 20:28)

- One section per theme, grouping the papers that belong together and keeping
  them coherent (20:42-21:31).
- **Reread the articles and revise the annotations**, again and again,
  especially the first ones read; each reading shows something missed before
  (21:41-22:26).
- What matters is how the papers relate: "these two share the data but differ in
  method; these share the method but not the data" (22:28-23:03). A section is
  about its theme, and the papers are the evidence for the critique of that
  theme, not a list of papers one by one (slide 14).
- Critique comes from comparing papers (23:03-23:47): a study on 100 patients
  looks good until you find studies on thousands; then you check why it had
  only 100 before you count it against it.

### Stage V: integrate sections (slide 15, from 24:10)

Tie the sections into one story: an introduction to the whole review, the
sections, and a conclusion that states the gap and what you will contribute to
it, which leads into the methodology (24:10-25:02).

## Writing it up (part 2)

**Three parts, written after the sections exist** (slide 16, 00:24-01:13):
introduction, body, conclusion.

- **Introduction** (slide 17, 01:14-02:42): the general topic and why people work
  on it; the overall trends (his example: some studied it from the management
  side, some from the legal side, nobody from the financial side); conflicts in
  theory or method; the main contributors; then your own point of view, what the
  picture lacks.
- **Body** (slide 18, 02:42-05:17): the sections from stage IV, grouped by
  **common denominators**, the themes that dominate the literature: qualitative
  against quantitative approaches, the method, the authors' conclusions, the
  specific purposes or objectives, chronology. Summarise individual studies, but
  above all summarise the whole body of opinion, comparatively. Use **umbrella
  sentences**: "the first three papers approach it from angle X; the fourth takes
  a different angle." The body is the longest part.
- **Conclusion** (slide 19, 05:18-07:10): the major contributions; the **current
  state of the art**, the best existing answer to the same problem, because "we
  start where the others ended" and do not reinvent the wheel (05:50-06:38); your
  insights; and **the gap**, said explicitly even though the slide does not say
  it, and only the gap you are going to address (06:49-07:10).

**A question from the class, and his answer** (07:12-08:05). A student asked
whether something already applied in practice, but not studied scientifically
with a before-and-after comparison, can still be a gap. His answer: yes. "When
is it not a gap? When it has been discussed scientifically. If it has not been
studied scientifically, it is still a gap": people do it by improvisation, and
nobody knows whether it is right.

## Rules while reading and writing (part 2, slides 20-23)

| rule | what it means | where |
|---|---|---|
| Be specific and succinct | State a paper's specific findings and methods; a review is not the place for long quotes or deep analysis of each point. Reading leads to more reading, so stay clear and stay on topic | slide 20, 08:05-08:31 |
| Be selective | With too many papers, keep the most important points: the ones most cited, most reviewed, most discussed | slide 20, 08:31-08:55 |
| Focus on current topics | Is the paper current? If not, has later work superseded it, or is it there for historical background? Do not drift to other topics | slide 20, 08:55-09:19 |
| Ensure evidence for claims | Every sentence in the review rests on a paper that says it; no claim from yourself (his example: "doctors resist it", says who?). On the slide: what evidence, of what type (experimental, statistical, anecdotal), is it relevant and sufficient, are the assumptions warranted | slide 21, 09:19-10:24 |
| Focus on sources of evidence | Cite the **original source**, the one the others cite, with high reliability (many citations). His example: "no lectures tomorrow", heard from a colleague who heard it from the dean, who received the circular: the dean is the source | slide 21, 10:24-12:30 |
| Account of contrary evidence | Where authors disagree, show the conflict and check it | slide 22, 12:30-12:57 |
| Reference citation | Every reference cited is in the reference list; nothing in the list that is not cited | slide 22, 12:57-13:10 |
| Avoid abbreviations and jargon | Spell out an abbreviation at first use; explain technical terms, since not every reader is from your field | slide 22, 13:10-13:44 |
| Simple, accurate sentences | Simple, correct, revised two or three times | slide 22, 13:46-13:58 |
| Organise by subtopic | A review is organised by subtopic, not by reference; one source may appear in several paragraphs | slide 23, 13:58-14:34 |
| Refer to the original source | As with the dean: trace an idea to where it began and cite that | slide 23, 14:38-14:47 |

## His assignments (for the course; recorded here for their numbers)

- Part 1 (slide 7, 11:31): list computing research databases (at least 2),
  journals (at least 10, ranked) and conferences (at least 5, ranked), with
  details for each. Where to search (slides 5-6): Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore,
  ACM, ScienceDirect, the Saudi Digital Library, Springer, PubMed, ERIC; in
  Google Scholar, the "cited by" count shows how much weight a paper carries
  (10:44-11:02), and the year filter narrows the search.
- Part 2 (slide 24, 14:57-15:58): choose a topic and write a review of about 15
  works by the five stages (annotate each, organise by theme, reread and refine,
  integrate), 1-2 pages, then a report on how the stages were followed. He would
  rather have 50 papers: start with 15 and add through "more reading" until about
  50.

## From the method to our tracker

Every column below comes from a point above. The 40 of `TOP40.md` are the
starting reading list; `TOP40.md`'s sections (M1-M7, L1-L9) are the first themes,
and stage II says themes come out of the reading, so the theme is a column the
reader can change.

**Filled in advance, from `TOP40.md` and `READING_LIST.md`:** ID; citation; link
and free full-text link; year (stage II orders a theme by date); access route
(FREE, SDL, BUY), and whether the team already holds the PDF
(`PAID_SOURCES_CHECK.md`); chapter (Methodology or Literature Review); theme
(the section); and "Read it for", the question `TOP40.md` sets for that paper.

**Filled by the reader:**

| column | from |
|---|---|
| Reader (one named team member; `READING_LIST.md` rule 4) | `READING_LIST.md` |
| Status: not started, abstract and conclusion read, read in full, annotated, reread and revised, cited | stage I's reading order (13:25-13:45); stage IV's rereading (21:41) |
| Related? after the abstract and conclusion: yes, no, unsure | 12:27-12:49, 13:25-13:35 |
| Source type: journal paper, conference paper, thesis, book, review article, standard or report, patent, press or web page | slide 4 (primary and secondary sources) |
| Method: theory or proof, simulation, measurement or experiment, review, standard or guideline, product | common denominators (part 2, 02:42-04:06); same method (14:14) |
| System studied: the plant or data (a diesel turbocharger, a building, a broiler house) | same data, same problem (14:14-14:42) |
| Critical synopsis, in your own words, 2-4 sentences: what it did, what it found, how good it is | stage I, slide 9 |
| Specific finding, with its page | be specific (slide 20) |
| Evidence: its type (measured, statistical, simulated, analytical, anecdotal), and whether it is sufficient | slide 21 |
| Strengths and weaknesses | slide 3; critique by comparison (23:03-23:47) |
| Current, superseded, or historical background | slide 20 |
| Contrary evidence: does it address work that disagrees? | slide 22 |
| Bears on our claim: supports it, challenges it, already does part of it (prior art), background only, not related; and why | known and not known, the gap (03:59-04:30); placing our work (05:36-06:04) |
| The answer to "Read it for", with the page | `TOP40.md` |
| Original source: if the idea we need is cited from elsewhere, which paper to cite instead | sources of evidence (10:24-12:30) |
| Leads to: authors, methods or papers worth reading next | stage III (slide 12) |
| Cited in the thesis, and where | reference citation (slide 22) |

**Two kinds of field.** Short, filterable fields (reader, status, related, type,
method, current, bears on our claim, cited) are columns. The long ones (the
synopsis, the evidence, strengths and weaknesses, the answer to "Read it for",
contrary evidence, leads to) are notes, one block per paper. That is why a
database with one page per paper (Notion) suits this better than one row of a
spreadsheet; in a spreadsheet the long fields become cells too narrow to read.

**One rule the tracker cannot replace.** A row filled from an abstract, a summary
or an AI's reading is not a read paper (`READING_LIST.md` rule 4). The status
"read in full" is set by the person who read it.

## The tracker as built (11 November 2026)

Jad chose Notion. The database "Literature Tracker · متتبع الأوراق" sits under
the team's GRAD Project Dashboard: one page per paper, the 40 of `TOP40.md`, all
"Not started", with no reader yet (splitting the papers is Jad's decision).

- **Filled in advance** from `TOP40.md` and `READING_LIST.md`: ID, citation, link
  and free full text, year, access route, chapter, theme (the `TOP40.md`
  section), "Read it for", and the source type, read off the citation. The PDF
  column follows `PAID_SOURCES_CHECK.md`: 9 in Ghassan's zip, D01, A03 and C36
  missing (each of those three pages carries a note), the rest free online. Each
  page's body holds "Why it is in the 40": what the list expects the source to
  support, not yet checked against its text.
- **Filled by the reader:** every column of the table above, grouped on each page
  as Reading, What we learned, Our claim, Classify, The source and Thesis.
  The views: All papers, What we learned, By theme (grouped by theme, oldest
  first, as stage II orders a theme), Reading board (by status) and By reader.
  The database's description repeats the reading order in English and Arabic.
- **Notes go in the columns, never in the page body.** The workspace is on
  Notion's free plan. When the 40 pages went in, Notion reported the workspace out
  of free blocks, with three days of grace (to 14 November) before an upgrade is
  needed to add content. Every paragraph and heading in a page body is a block;
  the pages first held 12 each, about 480 in all. Jad chose to slim them rather
  than pay or move to Google Sheets: the reading fields became columns, which are
  stored on the page itself and, by Notion's account of what a block is, add no
  block (not measured here); and the 9 empty template blocks per page were
  removed, about 360 in all, leaving a heading and a paragraph per page.
  Whether the workspace is back under the limit was not measured: Notion shows
  no block count through the connector.
